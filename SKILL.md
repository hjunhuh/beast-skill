# beast-skill

This skill turns the principles of the 36-page document that MrBeast (Jimmy Donaldson) wrote for new hires at his production company into working procedures you can apply directly to the user's YouTube videos. Do not quote the document at length. Restate each principle in your own words and apply it to the user's situation.

## Premises

1. **It is not a rulebook.** The author states plainly that the document is not a set of rules to follow literally and that everything should be applied with judgment for the situation. So do not mechanically run through checklists. Understand which metric each principle protects, then decide how it applies to the user's video.
2. **The goal is the best YouTube video.** Not the best-produced video, the funniest video or the highest-quality video. The test is whether YouTube viewers click and watch to the end. When the user is torn between cinematic craft and YouTube performance, frame the choice with this goal.
3. **The numbers are MrBeast's channel numbers.** An average length of 13 minutes 37 seconds and re-engagements at minutes 3 and 6 are that channel's data points. For other lengths and scales, convert them with the scaling rules below, and mark converted values as this skill's adaptation, not the document's text.
4. **Reflect what changed after 2024.** In March 2024 Donaldson said publicly that over the previous year he had slowed his videos down, focused on story and added breathers between scenes. So do not propose hyper-fast retention editing (a cut every second, nonstop sound effects, constant shouting) as the default. Keep the document's structure (first minute, re-engagements, back half, abrupt ending) but set cutting pace by story density.
5. **Some parts are deliberately not adopted.** The document includes passages about refusing to accept outside refusals and about pulling all-nighters before shoots. This skill only goes as far as politely asking another contact or another location for an alternative, and always respects clear refusals, filming permits, likeness rights, and the safety and rest of everyone on set. It also does not use the document's practice of grading employees into tiers in any production advice.

## Three metrics and one untrackable factor

Tie every recommendation to the metric it protects or improves. When users understand why, they can apply the principle themselves on the next video.

| Metric | Definition | Mainly decided by |
|---|---|---|
| CTR (click-through rate) | Share of people who saw the thumbnail and clicked. 100 million impressions and 10 million clicks is 10% | The appeal of the topic itself, thumbnail, title |
| AVD (average view duration) | How long viewers watch on average | First minute, structure, editing |
| AVP (average view percentage) | Average share of the video watched | Length decision and overall structure |
| Wow factor (untrackable) | The impression left by seeing something no other creator can do | Memory of the channel, watching the next video |

On AVP, the document says length is usually decided by Donaldson or the head of creative, so staff should focus on making people watch as long as possible. If the user is a solo creator, they decide the length too, so advise them to make the video as long as the content can hold attention and no longer.

## Choosing a work mode

Pick the mode based on what the user brings. If several apply, run them in order starting from A. When key information such as video length, title or thumbnail is missing, ask at most one or two questions and proceed with clearly stated assumptions for the rest.

| What the user brings | Mode |
|---|---|
| An idea, a topic, a channel direction | A. Packaging-first planning |
| A plan, a script, a shot outline | B. Minute-by-minute section map |
| A request for an editor brief, a cut, a timeline, a shot list | C. Edit notes |
| A retention graph, AVD, AVP, drop-off points | D. Retention diagnosis |
| A sponsored video, product placement | E. Sponsor integration |
| A shoot schedule, vendors, location scouting, team operations | F. Production operations |

## Mode A. Packaging-first planning

MrBeast's production starts with the title and thumbnail. The title and thumbnail set the viewer's expectations and the first minute exists to confirm them, so you cannot design the opening without knowing the packaging. Do the following before any script or shoot plan.

1. **For the same effort, pick the more extreme topic.** The document's example: spending 50 hours in a front yard and spending 50 hours in a bathtub of ketchup take about the same time and effort, but the second spreads far more. For each of the user's ideas, offer two or three more visual and more extreme versions at a similar cost.
2. **Raise the intensity of the title wording.** "I survived" beats "I spent", and "Bananas are the worst food on Earth" beats "I don't like bananas". Offer title candidates in the user's language that compare the strength of verbs and modifiers. Never propose exaggerations the video cannot deliver, because broken expectations cause viewers to leave.
3. **Make thumbnail elements match what is actually in the video.** The document's example: if the thumbnail shows a giant yellow bouncy castle next to skyscrapers, but the video shows a red one in a field and it is not the world's largest, viewers feel lied to and click away. Register every color, size, background, person and prop in the thumbnail as a critical component that must appear in the video as shown.
4. **Keep it simple.** The document's position is that something must be simple for tens of millions of people to understand it (and it adds that this, too, should be applied with judgment). Test whether the concept fits in one sentence, and cut it down if it does not.
5. **Choose one wow factor.** The document's example: in a video about living in a circle for 100 days, instead of starting with the contestant's house already in the circle, they brought it in on a crane about 30 seconds in. It looks inefficient by the data, but it leaves the impression that only this channel can do this. At the user's scale, propose wow moments built from expertise, access, relationships, sheer effort or unusual locations instead of money.
6. **List the critical components.** These are the things without which the video does not exist: the island in an island giveaway video, the props in the thumbnail, the challenges the creator singled out as the reason the idea works. Tell the user to check feasibility for each critical component first. The point of the feasibility stage is to stop before anyone else spends time on an idea that cannot happen.
7. **Creativity over money.** The document's example: as a prize for a gaming video, a year's supply of Doritos (5 bags a day x 365 days = 1,825 bags, about $1,825) is funnier and far cheaper than $20,000. For every large budget line, offer a cheaper and more entertaining alternative. If a big expense will never be shown on camera, point out that it should be reconsidered (the document's threshold is $10,000 or more).
8. **Find someone who has done it before.** To make the world's largest slice of cake, start by calling the person who made the previous record. Name specific experts or people with precedent the plan needs. The document calls consultants cheat codes.
9. **Back up coin-flip segments.** Where a segment has a roughly fifty-fifty chance of working, prepare an alternative idea alongside it.
10. **Safety and permits are critical components too.** For physical concepts such as survival, extreme environments or long endurance challenges, add stop criteria decided before the shoot (in what situations do we stop immediately) and safety measures to the critical components table. An endurance rule is a device for tension, not a rule worth risking your body for, and a stopped attempt is still content. Present rules about filming permits, camping, drones, foraging, currency imagery and similar as items to verify, and do not state unverified dates or clauses as fact. If web search is available, verify them and cite the source.
11. **Pick a format.** Recommend a fitting format from the table below, and advise against uploading the same format back to back; put other videos in between. The document's view is that viewers seem to want the same format forever, but actually want new things, and channels that rehash formats for years eventually die.

| Format | Structure | Why retention holds |
|---|---|---|
| Last to Leave | Several people endure and the last one standing wins | The winner is revealed only at the end |
| Stair Stepping | Escalating stakes, such as fireworks worth $1, $10, $50, $375, $1,000 and $10,000, ending with a world record | Stakes rise the deeper you go |
| Chase | The creator is hunted and either caught or not | The outcome stays open until the end |

**Mode A output template**

```
## Packaging
- Title candidates (by intensity): 1) ... 2) ... 3) ...
- Thumbnail composition: main subject / background / colors / text (if any)
- The promise the thumbnail makes: what the viewer will expect, in one line

## Stronger versions of the idea
| Original | More extreme version | Extra cost |

## Wow factor
- What, at which minute, and why only this channel can do it

## Critical components and feasibility
| Critical component | What happens without it | What to verify | Backup |

## Creativity over money
| Expensive item | Cheaper, more entertaining alternative |

## Who to call first (consultants)
## Format recommendation and why
```

## Mode B. Minute-by-minute section map

The document says that everyone, whether in production, creative or editing, must always know which minute of the video they are working on, and that if they do not, they are not doing it right. So always build the structure as a minute-by-minute map.

### The 13-minute prototype (document)

| Section | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00-1:00 First minute | Confirm the title and thumbnail promise immediately. Light the opening well, have the host prepare what to say, and pull the most interesting moments forward (front-loading) | Nearly every video loses the most viewers here. In the document's example, 21 million of 60 million viewers who clicked left within the first minute, which was still better than other channels |
| 1:00-3:00 | Stop telling viewers what they will see and start showing them. Crazy progression: in a video about surviving weeks in the woods, cover several days in the first three minutes instead of going day by day | Gets viewers invested in the story fast |
| About 3:00 Re-engagement 1 | A spectacle that fits the story and genuinely impresses. A segment that makes people think only this channel can do this | Catches viewers who would get bored and leave at this point |
| 3:00-6:00 | The most exciting content that is also very simple, quick scene changes, stimulating scenes that reflect the story. Make viewers fall in love with the people and the story | Viewers who watch the first half are very likely to finish |
| About 6:00 Re-engagement 2 | Something interesting that needs a bit more explanation and pushes the story into the back half | Momentum into the back half |
| 6:00-ending Back half | Viewers are in a lull, watching without noticing they are watching. Weaker material and long explanations go here. Things that went wrong can become content. Never signal the end unless it builds hype for the prize or payoff | The document warns that many videos died because the back half was bad |
| Ending | Cut abruptly right after a satisfying payoff | Dragging the ending collapses the end of the retention graph |

### Scaling to other lengths

The document only gives the 13-minute average. Scale other lengths proportionally as below, and label the results as scaled values in the output.

- For a video of L minutes, the first minute stays fixed, re-engagement 1 sits at about L x 0.23 and re-engagement 2 at about L x 0.46 (the ratios of 3 and 6 minutes in a 13-minute video).
- If L is under 8 minutes, confirm the promise within the first 30 to 45 seconds, put re-engagement 1 at the one-quarter mark and re-engagement 2 at the halfway mark. If the two land too close together, merge them.
- If L is over 20 minutes, keep the first-half structure and suggest adding a mid-point payoff in the back half to hold tension until the final payoff, while stating that this extends beyond the document.
- Shorts are a format the document does not cover. Use only the packaging-match and abrupt-ending principles as references, and do not apply the minute-by-minute structure.
- According to the document, real-time shoots and emotional videos run shorter and giant spectacles run longer. Fit the length itself to the nature of the content.

### Worked example (12 minutes, "Surviving 7 days on convenience store food only", re-engagements are scaled values)

| Timecode | Section | Scene | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:20 | First minute | A mountain of seven days' worth of convenience store food, framed like the thumbnail, and the rules in one sentence | Confirm the promise (AVD) |
| 0:20-1:00 | First minute | Quick previews of the three most dramatic moments of the seven days | Front-loading |
| 1:00-2:45 | Minutes 1-3 | Days 1 to 3 compressed, with day captions for a sense of progress | Crazy progression |
| About 2:45 | Re-engagement 1 | An unreleased lunch box made by a convenience store product developer | Access only this channel has |
| 2:45-5:30 | Minutes 3-6 | Days 4 and 5, short scene changes, changes in energy and health | Viewers grow attached to the host |
| About 5:30 | Re-engagement 2 | A new rule from day 6: a daily budget cap (needs some explaining) | Momentum into the back half |
| 5:30-11:40 | Back half | Explaining the medical checkup, the day that went wrong | Digested while viewers are in the lull |
| 11:40-12:00 | Ending | Cut right after the checkup results are revealed | Abrupt ending |

**Mode B output template**

```
## Section map (L minutes total, re-engagement points are scaled values)
| Timecode | Section | Scenes | Purpose (metric) | Footage needed |
## First-minute script draft (first line, first shot, when each thumbnail element appears)
## Two re-engagement devices: what, why it impresses, prep cost
## What goes in the back half and an end-signal check
## Ending: the payoff shot and the final cut
```

## Mode C. Edit notes

Use this mode to write an editor brief or to give feedback on a cut (a timeline, shot list, script or description). Write every note as "timecode + section name + what to do + why (which metric)". Notes without reasons cannot be generalized by the editor to the next video.

Review order:

1. **Speed of confirming the promise.** How fast does the opening show the title and thumbnail promise? Find anything that delays it (long intros, logos, channel introductions, background context) and move it back or cut it down.
2. **Brightness of the opening.** The document says they found a correlation between better lighting at the start of a video and less early drop-off. If the opening is dark, call for a correction.
3. **Thumbnail elements on screen.** Do the colors, props and people from the thumbnail appear early, exactly as shown?
4. **Switch to showing.** If teasers like "next, we're going to..." continue past the first minute, replace them with the actual scenes.
5. **Progression speed.** Is enough time compressed into minutes 1 to 3 (several days, several stages)?
6. **Re-engagement placement.** Do the devices actually sit at the target times on the section map? If they slipped, pull them earlier.
7. **No dull moments.** Does each cut push the story forward? Mark cuts that do not as removal candidates. However, following premise 4, keep short breathers between scenes, reaction shots and emotional moments, and do not confuse them with dullness.
8. **Minutes 3 to 6 scene changes.** This stretch calls for quick scene changes and simple, stimulating content.
9. **Back half quality.** Are long explanations gathered in the back half, and is there still no stretch below standard?
10. **Remove end signals.** Cut anything that signals the end, such as "finally", "to wrap up" or early outro music, unless it builds anticipation for the payoff.
11. **Ending.** Does the video end right after the payoff? A long outro, repeated goodbyes or subscribe requests tacked on after the payoff will collapse the end of the graph.
12. **Turn accidents into content.** If moments where things went wrong on the shoot were cut, check whether they are worth putting back. The document's attitude is that if an accident is funny, mention it in the video.
13. **Authenticity.** No staged fake reactions and no edits that make the host or participants look like they are lying. The document holds that intensity cannot be faked.
14. **Natural cast.** Lean toward keeping the cast's goofing around and silliness.
15. **Sponsor segment.** Review it against Mode E.

**Mode C output template**

```
## Summary (3 lines max): the biggest retention risk and the strongest section
## Edit notes
| Timecode | Section | Instruction | Reason (metric) | Priority (must / should / nice to have) |
## Gap against the section map: actual vs target positions of re-engagements 1 and 2
## Removal candidates and total time saved
## 1 to 3 rules to apply on the next video too
```

When writing an editor brief from scratch, put the Mode B section map before this template and add a one-line purpose for each section so the editor can check every cut against it.

## Mode D. Retention diagnosis

Diagnose when the user provides graph values, a description of a screenshot, AVD, AVP or retention by section.

1. **Start with retention at the one-minute mark.** The document's example (about 39 million of 60 million viewers still watching at one minute, roughly 65%) was better than average even on MrBeast's channel. Do not use it as an absolute benchmark. Compare against the user's own past videos.
2. **Match each sharp drop to what was on screen at that moment.** Common causes are scenes that differ from the thumbnail promise, stretches of teasers only, long explanations, sponsor reads that sound scripted, end signals, and outros that drag after the ending.
3. **Small AVD gaps matter.** In the document's example, two videos of almost the same length differed by 1 minute 38 seconds in average view duration, and their views were 120 million versus 45 million, nearly three times apart.
4. **Compare like with like.** The document compares videos using first-day data. Tell the user not to mix numbers taken at different times after upload.
5. **Early rank after upload.** YouTube Studio shows how a new video's early performance ranks against the channel's recent videos. The document calls this "out of 10", where 1 out of 10 is good and 10 out of 10 is bad.

**Mode D output template**

```
## Summary: the biggest loss and its likely cause
| Timecode | Retention change | What was on screen | Likely cause | Fix for the next video |
## First-minute assessment (vs the channel's past videos)
## Structural diagnosis: did the graph hold at the re-engagement points?
## 1 or 2 things to test on the next video
```

When data is missing, explain what to check in the audience retention report in YouTube Studio analytics, and only infer from the information given. Label inferences as inferences.

## Mode E. Sponsor integration

The document explains that old-style sponsor reads, where the host reads a script, left a crater in the retention graph, and that weaving the sponsor into the content made the dip far gentler. The principle is that brand deals are content.

- Design the sponsor's product to play a role in the story (a tool, a prize, an obstacle, the subject of a challenge).
- Decide the position on the section map. By default leave the first minute and the re-engagement devices untouched, and state that this placement rule is this skill's judgment, since the document does not specify a position.
- Check that the transitions into and out of the sponsor segment feel natural.
- A reference for persuading advertisers: according to the document, one sponsor's CEO said the return on a MrBeast integration was 1.7 times that of an NFL championship game ad, and that each new upload sent viewers back to the previous video, bringing customers back again.

**Output:** 2 or 3 sponsor integration options (position, approach, draft script, expected retention impact)

## Mode F. Production operations

A large part of the document covers pre-shoot operations. If the user works with a team or vendors, turn the following into concrete actions. For solo creators, scale them down to their vendors, locations and people on camera.

1. **Name the bottleneck.** When you hand off work your own task depends on, such as a thumbnail sketch or the creative plan, never stop at "let me know when it's done". Tell the person they are the bottleneck and why, agree on a completion date, and check progress every day until then.
2. **Do not hand off and forget vendors.** Set a completion date well ahead of the shoot, prepare a backup in parallel if it is a critical component, and get daily progress videos. You need to know about problems weeks ahead; days ahead is too late.
3. **Treat critical components obsessively.** Check them several times a day, prepare backups, pick up shipments yourself instead of trusting standard delivery, and speak up the moment something goes wrong. Leaving a critical component to chance means leaving the whole video to chance.
4. **Video everything.** Record scouting trips, sets and ordered items on video instead of relying on one photo or memory, so the whole team sees the same picture.
5. **Say the negatives.** Do not report only the good parts. Include why something might not work (availability, budget overruns, risks).
6. **Bring context and options to every decision request.** Use the template below.
7. **Match the channel to the message.** The more complex and urgent the issue, the higher the form of communication: in person, then a call, then a message, then email. Written communication only counts once the other person confirms they read it.
8. **Communication lines.** When you ask someone in another department for work, tell their lead first. Each task should have exactly one owner, or work gets duplicated.
9. **Backup day.** Secure the option to extend locations and rentals by a day. The document advises choosing a 90% great option that can be extended over a 100% great option with no flexibility.
10. **Verify deals that look too good.** If everyone else says no and one vendor says yes, dig into why.
11. **Protect your priority list.** Your assigned priorities come before other requests.
12. **Work on several videos every day.** Going all in on one video for days wipes out the lead time on the others and starts a vicious cycle.
13. **On set.** Remove anyone who is not needed so participants can act naturally. Never leave participants waiting in places like direct sun for more than three hours (the document cites cases where breaking this proved very costly). Prepare participants ahead of time so they feel comfortable talking.
14. **Rendering terms.** Call a render a pre-vis only if that is what will actually be built. Call inspiration renders concepts.
15. **Five problem-solving tools.** First classify which of math, science, vision, approvals or budget solves the problem.
16. **Reputation check.** Do not do anything that could hurt the channel from a PR perspective.
17. **Handling refusals.** Per premise 5, go no further than politely asking for alternatives.

**Decision request template** (generalized from the document's car example)

```
[Video and position] The [X] video, the [X] scene in minutes 6 to 9
[Budget] [X] allocated, can go up by [X] (confirmed with [X])
[Search scope] Checked [X] vendors in [X] area
[Options] 5 options pre-approved by creative + 5 backup options in a different direction, each with photos and key details
[Question] Which of these is best, or should I find more options?
```

Bad example: "We're thinking of using [X] in the next video, what do you think? It costs [X]." (No context, no alternatives, nothing confirmed.)

**Mode F output:** a countdown schedule from the shoot date (D-45, D-30, D-14, D-7, D-1) with the bottleneck at each point, a critical components tracker, and daily check-in items.

## Shared creative principles

Use these as background principles in every mode.

- **The host must be excited.** The document defines the goal of the content as exciting the host himself. If the host is not dying to get in front of the camera, authenticity disappears, and authenticity is part of why the channel succeeds. When evaluating a plan, ask the user whether they can hardly wait to shoot it.
- **Everything must keep getting better.** Camera angles, pacing, story, jokes, color, lighting, music, props, people, framing, ideas. Suggest one thing to improve over the last video.
- **Anything can be content.** The document says even a single baby doll yields variations such as throwing it as far as possible with your weak hand, getting 1,000 of them into a distant crib, or seeing how many it takes to break a two-by-four. Generate at least five variations from one of the user's topics.
- **Define the viewer in one sentence.** The document describes the channel's average viewer as a teenage memer who likes video games. Write the same kind of definition for the user's channel and fit every judgment to it.
- **Information diet.** What planners and writers consume shapes their ideas, because you cannot be inspired by things you do not know exist. If the user says they are out of ideas, propose a daily consumption list suited to their genre.
- **Watch your own channel.** The document asks new hires to watch the last 50 videos. Advise users who hire a new editor or writer to make an onboarding watch list.
- **Show it to many people.** Show ideas to as many people as possible and ask how to make them better.
- **Famous people.** If a guest is gettable, their name value is part of the wow too.

## Output rules

- Reply in the user's language.
- When the user asks for several deliverables at once (titles, structure, brief and so on), chain them in mode order, and keep anything meant to be handed to someone else, such as an editor brief, in one block that can be copied as is.
- State any assumptions you made for missing information (number of people on camera, shoot timing, sponsorship and so on) briefly at the top.
- Attach a timecode or section name to every time-related recommendation.
- Distinguish content from the document from what this skill scaled or extended (for example "document", "scaled", "adapted").
- Do not present MrBeast's channel numbers as targets for the user's channel. When scale and budget differ, take only the principle.
- Do not quote the document at length. Explain principles in your own words and use only short phrases where they are essential.
- End each deliverable with 1 to 3 rules the user can reuse on the next video as is. The long-term goal of this skill is for the user to internalize the principles.
